What dessert catering
actually covers.

Start here

Dessert catering is the planned sweet course of an event. At one end it is the single plated dessert that comes with a package; at the other it is a dedicated bar, station or buffet with its own styling, staff and equipment, quoted on top of the meal. In the Philippines the sweet course runs from leche flan and kakanin trays at family gatherings to styled dessert bars at weddings and debuts, and it is the part of the menu guests photograph first.

When does a dedicated sweet course make sense? Three honest answers: when the package dessert is a single slice and the celebration wants a table, when the program has a segment that needs a backdrop (an 18 roses table, a cake cutting corner), and when guests eat across several hours, which a plated dessert cannot serve. When the event is short and the meal is heavy, the package dessert is enough; adding a bar for the sake of it adds a line to the quote without adding to the room.

The rest of this guide walks the three set ups, the menu, the portions and the quote, so you can decide which end of that range fits your celebration and order it with the rest of the food in one place.

Three set ups:
bar, station, buffet.

Pick the shape

The three dessert set ups differ in who serves and how much styling they carry, and the difference decides the quote:

Dessert buffet catering. The long shared table: sweets arranged on risers and trays, guests serve themselves, and the table is restocked through the event. It suits big guest lists and family celebrations, and it is the most forgiving set up for kakanin, bars and fruit. Our buffet and food station formats guide shows how it sits beside the main buffet line.

Dessert bar catering. The styled single table that doubles as decor: cupcakes on stands, shooters in glass, a small cake as the centerpiece. Guests pick up and go. A bar is the set up photographers love at weddings and debuts, and because it runs cold and unattended it prices below any staffed option.

Dessert station catering. The staffed counter where sweets are assembled in front of guests: halo-halo, crepes, waffles, milk tea or shaved ice. A station becomes part of the program, not just the menu, which is why it prices highest: each one carries an attendant, live preparation and its own equipment. One station anchors an event; three stations need the floor space to match.

A working rule: bars for looks, buffets for volume, stations for the show. Most celebrations quote one of the three, and the biggest ones quote a bar plus a single station and let the two carry the program together.

Building a dessert catering menu
guests actually finish.

Four to six items

A dessert catering menu works best at four to six items, and the mix matters more than the count: one or two chilled Filipino favorites, one or two handheld sweets, one kakanin tray and one fruit-based option. That spread covers the guests who want a spoon, the guests who want something they can eat standing, and the guests who will ask for something their grandmother would recognize.

The Filipino sweets that earn their spot at real events: leche flan, buko pandan, mango float in cups, mini brazo de mercedes, sapin-sapin, bibingka, puto bumbong in season, banana fritters, fruit tarts and halo-halo when there is a station to serve it. Dessert ideas that fail share one trait: too many melting-risk items outdoors. Menus for wedding catering add the cake cutting to the spread; menus for 18th debut catering lean into the styled table because the 18 roses program runs beside it.

Two planning rules from the events we serve. First, match the menu to the venue: chilled favorites for shaded or air conditioned rooms, kakanin and dry sweets for outdoor afternoons. Second, put the sweet course in the same conversation as the meal, not after it: the catering menu guide shows how dessert sits in the full course order, and one quote that covers both keeps the tables, the staff and the timing honest.

Easy catering desserts that
survive a Philippine afternoon.

Heat-proof picks

Easy catering desserts are not the lazy list; they are the ones that look as good at hour three as at hour one. The test is three questions: does it melt, does it portion itself, and does it survive a refuel without a chef. Sweets that pass all three: kakanin, brownies and bars, cookies, fruit tarts, cupcakes with firm frosting, and shooters.

Catering dessert ideas worth copying from the events we quote: shooters and cups first, because dessert shooters catering portions itself cleanly, one sealed cup per guest, no serving utensils and no crowding at the table. Kakanin trays second, restocked on schedule instead of displayed all at once. Brownies and bars third, cut small so guests take two instead of abandoning half of one.

For the chilled favorites, the logistics are the dessert: cold transport boxes, ice pans or a chiller table on site, and batches that come out in waves so nothing sits past two hours in the heat. A dessert bar that plans refills this way reads fresh all afternoon; one that displays everything at the gate reads tired by the program's middle. Confirm the refill schedule in the proposal, not in a text message on the event morning.

How dessert catering
is quoted.

No published prices

Dessert is quoted separately from the per-head menu rate, and for a reason that is fair once you see it: the sweet course brings its own line items. The quote moves with four things:

Pieces per head. Two to three pieces per guest when dessert is a highlight; one to two when the package already carries a plated dessert or a cake cutting. The piece count drives food cost more than any other choice you make.

Chilling. Cream-based sweets need cold transport and a cold display, and both appear on the quote as equipment and refills. Dry sweets and kakanin skip that layer entirely.

Staffing. A bar runs unattended; a station carries an attendant for every hour it operates. That line is labor, and it is the difference between the two set ups.

Styling and rentals. Risers, stands, jars, linens, signage and florals. A table that doubles as event decor is priced like decor because that is the job it is doing.

What we do not do is publish a per-head number, because an honest number depends on the four lines above plus your city and your date. The same is true of the whole menu: our pricing explainer shows every factor that moves a catering quote. Send the guest count, the venue and the date, and the quote you get back prices the sweet course with the meal, not as a surprise at the tasting.

Common questions.

Dessert catering FAQs

What is dessert catering?
Dessert catering is the dedicated sweet course of an event, planned and delivered by a caterer: from a plated dessert that comes with the package up to a full dessert bar or station with its own styling, staff and equipment. In the Philippines it usually covers cakes, cupcakes, kakanin, leche flan, fruit tarts and chilled Filipino favorites served alongside or instead of the main menu's dessert.

What is the difference between a dessert bar and a dessert station?
A dessert bar is a styled table of ready to eat sweets that guests pick up themselves, and it doubles as part of your event decor. A dessert station is served and assembled in front of guests: a halo-halo station, a crepe or waffle station, a milk tea or shaved ice counter. Bars cost less because they run cold and unattended; stations price higher because each one carries staff, live preparation and extra equipment.

What desserts work best for catering in the Philippines?
The best catering dessert ideas for a Philippine event are sweets that hold up outdoors and portion cleanly: leche flan, buko pandan, mango float cups, mini brazo de mercedes, kakanin such as sapin-sapin and bibingka, fruit tarts, brownies and bars, and dessert shooters in small cups. Save melting-risk desserts such as iced cakes with buttercream and ice cream scoops for air conditioned venues or shaded setups with chillers.

How many dessert pieces per person should I plan?
Plan 2 to 3 dessert pieces per guest when the sweet course is a highlight of the event, and 1 to 2 pieces per guest when your package already includes a plated dessert or a cake cutting. Shooters, cups and jars each count as one piece, and a candy or kakanin tray usually serves 10 to 15 guests. Your caterer should size the count against the guest list, the weather and the rest of the menu.

Does dessert catering need refrigeration?
Most of it does. Leche flan, buko pandan, mango float, cream-based shooters and fruit tarts need chilled transport and a cold display such as ice pans or a chiller table, and Philippine heat shortens safe display time to about two hours per batch. Kakanin, brownies and dry bars are the low-risk exceptions. Confirm the chilling plan and the refill schedule with your caterer before the event, not on the day.

Can dessert catering be added to an existing catering package?
Yes, and it is the usual way couples and planners do it: the base menu carries the meal and the dessert course is quoted as an add-on with its own pieces per head, styling and rentals. Because a dessert bar or station brings its own staff, equipment and decor, it is priced separately from the per-head menu rate. See the full catering menu guide to see where the sweet course fits.

How much does dessert catering cost in the Philippines?
There is no honest fixed number, which is why every page here is quote only. The quote moves with four things: how many pieces per head you want, whether the set up needs chilling, whether a station needs staff, and how much styling and rental furniture the table carries. A tray of kakanin and a styled halo-halo station with an attendant are different jobs. Send your guest count, venue and date and we quote the exact set up.

Do dessert bars work for kids' parties and debuts?
Both are natural fits. Kids' birthdays run on candy carts, cupcakes and shooters that portion themselves and survive small hands; debuts pair a dessert bar with the 18 roses program because the table doubles as styling for the photos. For corporate events, a coffee and dessert pairing table works afternoon events best. See our wedding catering and debut catering pages for how the sweet course sits in the program.
